# New-question first-reply experiments

The October 22 owner requested faster first replies to new questions in the separate practice copy, preserving Heart, the live client's setup and no API billing. Four candidate configurations were measured. **None established a useful, reliable improvement, so none was activated.** Both running apps, their settings and the previous source remain unchanged. First-question latency is unresolved.

All model requests used the existing exact owner-practice Pro binding in place, Sol/low and synthetic facts. Native speech used the pinned Heart checkpoint, normal speaking speed and existing test CPU settings. No API key, new provider, paid service, credential copy, live restart or care write was introduced. Foreground preparation, care authority and final response validation remained in force.

The [official OpenAI latency guidance](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/latency-optimization) supports evaluating shorter output and overlapping work. It does not establish that either improves this particular subscription route. Earlier low/none comparisons were already unsuccessful, so this experiment examined answer length and speech preparation instead.

The direct screen used six fixed new questions and two repetitions, reversing candidate order. Service comparisons used rain, sky and music questions with fresh conversations, actual care authority, read-only synthetic facts, the actual bound reasoner and native Heart. The two six-answer-per-path comparisons reversed order on the second repetition; the three-answer-per-path refinement had only one repetition. Earlier rejected results were retained rather than combined into a favorable aggregate.

Timing starts at a typed request and ends when the first validated Heart waveform is available. It excludes microphone capture, transcription, remote browser transport and audible playback. All provider requests were sequential, and CPU tests/builds did not overlap the latency measurements. These are small engineering samples, not production percentiles. Provider first-text timing varied, and the apparent overall gain cannot be attributed to the audio change alone.

To isolate rendering, each of the twelve final-comparison answers was rendered at both part sizes, alternating size order. Those 24 native renderings used no additional model requests. Median rendering was 820.5 ms at 72 characters and 804.5 ms at 48 characters: only **16 ms** improvement. This does not substantiate a meaningful conversational speed gain. Shorter replies also tended to lose existing speech/generation overlap; generating fewer words did not reliably make the first audible answer ready sooner.

Two authenticated questions on the unchanged test site took **3.681 s and 3.890 s** to first validated audio. All 22 HTTP checks passed, including independent test login/cookie, the synthetic profile, read-only replies, complete 24 kHz WAV parts, playback acknowledgement and numeric model metadata. A separate foreground continuation preparation consumed one request. There was no after-activation phase because no candidate was selected, and neither app was restarted.

The accuracy review did not certify every science answer. Of six answers per path in the final comparison, three baseline answers and four candidate answers were fully supported under the precision rubric. Both candidate sky explanations gave the relevant scattering mechanism but incorrectly generalized the comparison with other colors; [NASA explains the blue/violet distinction](https://science.nasa.gov/ems/03_behaviors/). The baseline included similar wording, one answer describing sunlight as blue, and an approaching-raindrop explanation for smell before rainfall that was not established by the [fetched MIT mechanism study](https://news.mit.edu/2015/rainfall-can-release-aerosols-0114). These limitations remain open; no permanent factual correction or general source-connected answering mechanism was implemented.

Other reviewed explanations were checked against [NASA's raindrop description](https://gpm.nasa.gov/science/anatomy-of-a-raindrop), the MIT rainfall study and the [author-hosted music-memory research](https://pscresearch.faculty.ucdavis.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/112/2014/11/Janata-music-evoked-memories.pdf). The latter is a small study of young adults, not evidence of a treatment effect. Two additional hypothetical medication/emergency cases preserved the prescribed-dose boundary and prioritized urgent help. Both passed, with zero care writes; this is not clinical validation.

The rejected candidate passed 64 focused tests, including withheld tentative audio, final-text mismatch, incomplete/wrong-model/tool output, changed authority, default-off selection and text-preserving speech parts. After rejection, all 342 prior source files matched the previous manifest exactly; the two trial-only files were removed and private candidate sources/patches retained. The restored current revision passed typecheck and 51 focused regressions, with zero failures. The full suite was not rerun because no application change was retained; its earlier result is not counted as a new run.
